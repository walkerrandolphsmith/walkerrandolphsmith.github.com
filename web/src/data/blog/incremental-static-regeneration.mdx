**We build for users at scale, and they notice when it is slow.**

A Lighthouse score of 48 was more than a poor technical grade. It showed that our help experiences across apps were not fast, accessible, or visible enough in search.

The problem was not the content. It was how we delivered it.

## The Context: High Variation, High Stakes

Each help article had to adapt to several dimensions:

- Product
- Language and region
- Application version
- Platform (Windows, macOS, web)

The result was a very large number of permutations. For each request, the server evaluated the user context, got the right content, and merged it with UI logic to render the page.

We used runtime rendering for this. A web server delivered the pages, with edge caching and some in-memory optimizations. This gave us flexibility, but it had costs:

- JavaScript payloads were too large.
- Content moved during page load, which decreased visual stability.
- CDN caching could not handle the variety of content.
- Time to first byte was inconsistent and often poor.

Our architecture no longer met our needs.

## The Strategy: Leaning into Next.js ISR

We examined our delivery model again with one principle: **performance is a product feature, not a backend concern**.

Next.js **Incremental Static Regeneration (ISR)** gave us the model we needed. We could:

- Pre-render popular article permutations as static HTML.
- Regenerate pages on demand when traffic required it.
- Stop runtime merging for most content.

This would move load off our servers, simplify our caching, and improve the experience in desktop and web apps.

But we had a problem to solve first.

## The Challenge: Too Many Permutations to Prebuild

We could not prebuild every article variation. There were hundreds of thousands of possible combinations.

We needed a selective strategy to:

- Identify which permutations to build in advance.
- Handle less frequent variants without a decrease in performance.
- Prevent unnecessary rebuilds of content.

Our first implementation was simple: short TTLs, opportunistic revalidation, and minimal memory caching.

It helped, but it did not scale.

## What Worked: Observability-Informed Regeneration

We changed to a demand-driven model based on usage telemetry:

- **Hot Path Pre-rendering**: The top 5–10% of article permutations were built at deploy time or through background revalidation.
- **On-Demand ISR**: Rare combinations were rendered at first access and cached at the CDN. They could then be promoted.
- **Promotion via Usage Heuristics**: When users accessed an article frequently in a short time window, the system flagged it for regeneration and persistent caching.
- **Memory-Aware LRU Strategy**: We implemented an in-memory cache with tiered eviction. We optimized it for clusters of region and language.

We also added diagnostics and tracing. They monitored cache effectiveness, regeneration costs, and end-user performance.

## Outcomes: Not Just Faster, but Smarter

The change gave measurable improvements:

- **Lighthouse Score**: Increased from 48 to 100 across key user flows.
- **TTFB**: Decreased by more than 80% for high-traffic paths.
- **SEO**: Better crawlability and rankings for public articles.
- **Developer Velocity**: Simpler logic gave fewer edge cases and easier testing.

The architecture also became more predictable under load. Teams could trust the system again. That trust is critical when we ship help experiences at our scale.

## Lessons Learned

- **Static-first works, even with dynamic content, if you treat variation as a caching problem and not a rendering problem.**
- **Observability drives good architecture.** Our best decisions came from real usage data, not assumptions.
- **Performance is a team sport.** Development, infrastructure, SEO, and content authorship all contribute to the quality that users see.

## Closing Thoughts

This was more than a technical rewrite. It was a change in how we think. We moved from a reactive, request-based architecture to a proactive model based on usage.

The Lighthouse score is a good headline. But the real outcome was user trust: faster help, less friction, and an experience that scaled with the products it supports.

We learned that performance is about more than speed.  
It is about making things feel effortless.
