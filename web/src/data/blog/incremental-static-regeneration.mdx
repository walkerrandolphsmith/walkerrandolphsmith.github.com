We build for users at scale, and they notice when it is slow.

A Lighthouse score of 48 was more than a poor technical grade. It showed that our help experiences across apps were not fast, accessible, or visible enough in search.

The problem was not the content. It was how we delivered it.

## The Context: High Variation, High Stakes

Each help article had to adapt to the product, the language and region, the application version, and the platform, which could be Windows, macOS, or web. The result was a very large number of permutations. For each request, the server evaluated the user context, got the right content, and merged it with UI logic to render the page.

We used runtime rendering for this. A web server delivered the pages, with edge caching and some in-memory optimizations. This gave us flexibility, but it had costs. JavaScript payloads were too large, and content moved during page load, which decreased visual stability. CDN caching could not handle the variety of content, and time to first byte was inconsistent and often poor.

Our architecture no longer met our needs.

## The Strategy: Leaning into Next.js ISR

We examined our delivery model again with one principle: performance is a product feature, not a backend concern.

Next.js Incremental Static Regeneration (ISR) gave us the model we needed. We could pre-render popular article permutations as static HTML and regenerate pages on demand when traffic required it. For most content, we could stop runtime merging completely.

This would move load off our servers, simplify our caching, and improve the experience in desktop and web apps.

But we had a problem to solve first.

## The Challenge: Too Many Permutations to Prebuild

We could not prebuild every article variation. There were hundreds of thousands of possible combinations.

We needed a selective strategy. It had to identify which permutations to build in advance, handle less frequent variants without a decrease in performance, and prevent unnecessary rebuilds of content.

Our first implementation was simple: short TTLs, opportunistic revalidation, and minimal memory caching.

It helped, but it did not scale.

## What Worked: Observability-Informed Regeneration

We changed to a demand-driven model based on usage telemetry. An ensemble of strategies chose which content to prebuild. It picked the most referenced and most searched content, and it used create and update timestamps so that newer files stayed warm. The top 5–10% of article permutations were the hot path, and we built them at deploy time or through background revalidation. Rare combinations were rendered on demand at first access and cached at the CDN. When users accessed an article frequently in a short time window, the system flagged it for regeneration and persistent caching, which promoted it toward the hot path. Behind this, an in-memory LRU cache with tiered eviction kept memory use under control. We optimized it for clusters of region and language.

We also added diagnostics and tracing. They monitored cache effectiveness, regeneration costs, and end-user performance.

## Outcomes: Not Just Faster, but Smarter

The change gave measurable improvements. The Lighthouse score increased from **48 to 100** across key user flows. TTFB decreased by more than 80% for high-traffic paths. Public articles got better crawlability and rankings. Developer velocity also improved, because simpler logic gave fewer edge cases and easier testing.

The architecture also became more predictable under load. Teams could trust the system again. That trust is critical when we ship help experiences at our scale.

## Lessons Learned

Static-first works, even with dynamic content, if you treat variation as a caching problem and not a rendering problem.

Observability drives good architecture. Our best decisions came from real usage data, not assumptions.

Performance is a team sport. Development, infrastructure, SEO, and content authorship all contribute to the quality that users see.

## Closing Thoughts

This was more than a technical rewrite. It was a change in how we think. We moved from a reactive, request-based architecture to a proactive model based on usage.

The Lighthouse score is a good headline. But the real outcome was user trust, because help became faster and easier to use, and the experience scaled with the products it supports.

We learned that performance is about more than speed.  
It is about making things feel effortless.
