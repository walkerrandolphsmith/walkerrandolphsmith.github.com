This is the eleventh post in a series about designs the constraints chose for us, and it's a close-up on a moment everyone's build does and almost nobody examines: the instant a package-restore step authenticates to a private feed.

It looks trivial. Your `dotnet restore` or `npm install` needs to pull from a feed that requires a token, so you get a token and hand it to the build. Done. Except that single moment is three distinct security boundaries stacked on top of each other, and the typical write-up addresses one of them — badly. There's how you *obtain* the token, how you *pass* it to the build without it leaking, and what the token actually *unlocks*. A gap at every turn. This post is about navigating all three, and about the one that turns out not to be a secrets problem at all.

I touched the middle one in passing in [an earlier post](/blog/the-cutover-is-a-content-move) — switching a feed token from a Dockerfile `ENV` to a BuildKit secret. Here's the whole argument, including the part that elevates it from a hygiene tip to a supply-chain decision.

## Start with the question nobody asks: why is there a token at all?

Most treatments of feed authentication open with "the private feed needs a token, so here's how to shove it into the build." That framing throws away the most important thing in the picture. The token exists *because the feed is private*, and the feed is private **on purpose** — and the purpose is not to hide your code.

A build that restores from a public feed pulls whatever the open internet is serving at the moment `restore` runs: a typosquatted package name, a transitive dependency that got compromised last night, a version that was yanked and silently replaced. You have no say over what enters your build, because anyone can publish into the namespace you're pulling from.

A private feed inverts that. It's a perimeter for *packages*, in exactly the sense the [container registry is a perimeter for images](/blog/the-registry-is-the-perimeter): everything that can enter is something you admitted. You decide which upstream packages are mirrored in, you can scan them on the way through, you can hold them at a version, and you can prove the provenance of every artifact your build consumed. The token is the key to a gate — and the gate is the point. The how-to internet treats the gate as friction to get past. A principal treats it as the single most valuable control in the dependency supply chain.

So the reframe that gives this post its title: **private isn't about secrecy, it's about governance — and governance is what makes the code trustable, not merely private.** "We pulled our dependencies from a feed we control and curate" is a fundamentally different sentence, to an auditor and to yourself, than "we pulled them from wherever, over TLS." Same encryption on the wire. Completely different trust in what arrived.

## Trustable means pinned, not just gated

Gating *what's allowed in* is half of it. The other half is pinning *exactly what came in*, so the curated set is also a reproducible, tamper-evident one. That's the lock file, and it's why the real restore step looks like this:

```dockerfile
RUN --mount=type=secret,id=feed_token \
    FEED_TOKEN="$(cat /run/secrets/feed_token)" \
    dotnet restore --use-lock-file --verbosity quiet "src/EventManager/EventManager.csproj"
```

with the `packages.lock.json` copied in *before* the restore. `--use-lock-file` means the build restores an exact, hash-verified dependency closure: the same inputs produce the same bytes every time, and any drift between what's pinned and what the feed serves fails the build instead of silently resolving to something new. A private feed governs the set of packages allowed to exist; the lock file pins precisely which of them this build consumed and proves none were swapped. Curated *and* provable — that's the substance behind "trustable," and it's the same pin-it-and-prove-it discipline this series keeps landing on, now at the dependency layer.

That's the elevating frame. Now the concrete focus, because getting the trust model right means nothing if you leak the key to the gate into every copy of your image.

## The token must never land in a layer

Here's the part the how-tos get wrong, and it's the spine of this post. The obvious ways to get a token into a build all write it down permanently.

```dockerfile
# Both of these leak. Don't.
ENV FEED_TOKEN=eyJ0eX...            # baked into image config — visible in `docker inspect`, forever
ARG FEED_TOKEN                      # recorded in build history — visible in `docker history`
```

A Dockerfile `ENV` bakes the value into the image configuration, where it persists for the life of the image and surfaces under `docker inspect`. A `--build-arg` is recorded in the image's build history and surfaces under `docker history`. Build arguments are *not* secret; treating them as such is the single most common way credentials escape a build.

And here's the subtlety the multi-stage-build crowd misses: moving the token into an early build stage does **not** save you. "It's only in the build stage, not the final image" confuses *not in the final layers* with *never written down*. The credential still materialized in a layer that exists during the build — in that stage's history, in the build cache, in anything that snapshots intermediate stages. A leak you can't see in the final image is still a leak; you've just made it harder to notice, which is worse.

The fix is a BuildKit secret mount, and it's a genuinely different mechanism rather than a tidier version of the same one:

```bash
# in the build wrapper — the token becomes a file BuildKit mounts, never a build-arg
printf '%s' "$FEED_TOKEN" > "$TOKEN_FILE"
docker build --secret "id=feed_token,src=$TOKEN_FILE" ...   # DOCKER_BUILDKIT=1
trap 'rm -f "$TOKEN_FILE"' EXIT
```

`--mount=type=secret,id=feed_token` exposes the token as a file at `/run/secrets/feed_token` **only for the duration of the single `RUN` that asks for it**, on a tmpfs that is never committed to a layer. The restore command reads it, uses it, and the mount evaporates when the command finishes. It is not in the layer filesystem, not in `docker history`, not in `docker inspect`, not in the cache. The token did its one job and left no trace.

One detail in that build wrapper that's worth stealing: the secret is passed for *every* build, and Dockerfiles that don't reference `id=feed_token` simply ignore it. So a single build script enforces the secure path for every service — .NET, Node, anything — and no individual Dockerfile is trusted to remember to do it right. The secure way is the only way, centrally.

In the language of the [secrets-maturity model](/blog/its-not-where-the-secret-lives) I laid out earlier, this is the **exposure-surface** dial turned all the way down. A baked token's exposure is enormous: it travels in every pull of the image, sits in every registry that holds a copy, lives in every layer cache, and is readable by anyone who can run `docker history`. A secret-mounted token's exposure is "a tmpfs that existed for one `RUN` on one builder, and is gone." Same secret, radically smaller blast radius — without changing what the secret *is*.

## Pull back one more step: where did the token come from?

We've made sure the token doesn't *leak*. But the exposure dial isn't the only one, and a leak-proof token that is itself a static, long-lived credential has just moved the problem upstream. If your build reads a permanent feed PAT out of a CI variable, you've got a standing bearer secret sitting in your pipeline configuration, and the secret-mount fix did nothing for its lifespan.

So the mature move is to treat CI as a *system with an identity*, and have that identity mint a **short-lived** token at build time — no static PAT anywhere in the picture. In the build I'm drawing from, the wrapper calls a single `inject-feed-token.sh`, and that script is the *only* path to a token: it derives a short-lived credential from the CI identity — a service connection granted exactly the permission to read the feed — and everything downstream sees only the token's output, never a stored secret. One audited choke point for credential acquisition instead of N scattered copies of "get a token."

I'm deliberately keeping this provider-agnostic, because the specifics are the least interesting part and the pattern is the whole point. Ours happened to be an Azure DevOps service connection minting a token for an Azure Artifacts feed — but the move is identical whether your CI is GitHub Actions exchanging an OIDC token, GitLab, or anything else, and whether the private registry is a NuGet feed, a private npm registry, a PyPI index, or a container registry. An identity that's been granted a permission mints an ephemeral credential, instead of a human pasting a static PAT into a secret variable. That turns the **lifespan** and **attribution** dials: the token expires on its own, and every acquisition traces back to the pipeline identity that requested it rather than to a shared secret nobody can attribute.

## A gap at every turn — and you close each one

Step back and the "one trivial moment" of feed authentication is three boundaries in a row, and the value is in closing all three rather than one:

```text
1. ACQUIRE   an identity mints a short-lived token        ← not a static PAT in a CI variable   (lifespan dial)
2. PASS      a BuildKit secret mount, one RUN, no layer   ← not ENV / --build-arg                (exposure dial)
3. UNLOCK    a private, governed, lock-pinned feed        ← not the open internet                (trust: what may enter)
```

The how-to internet addresses, at most, the middle row, and usually gets it wrong. The principal-level posture closes all three, and they reinforce each other: the short-lived token bounds what a leak is worth, the secret mount keeps the leak from happening, and the governed feed bounds what a hostile dependency could even be. That's the same instinct that runs through this whole series — minimize standing credentials, shrink exposure, and put a governed boundary at every place something enters your system. A build is just another place something enters. The feed at the end of it is the boundary that quietly upgrades "our code" to "code we can vouch for."

## The honest edges

- **The secret mount protects persistence, not the running build.** The token is plaintext in tmpfs and in the restore process's environment *during* the `RUN`. A malicious package with a pre-install hook could read `/run/secrets` while it executes. That's not an argument against secret mounts — it's the argument for strictly scoping the token, because scope is what bounds the blast radius of exactly this attack. If the token only has *read* access to the feed, the worst a hijacked hook can do is pull more packages it could already see. If it has *publish* access, you've handed a supply-chain attacker the keys to your internal registry — they can poison the very feed every other build trusts. Same leak, two completely different incidents. It's also why topics 2 and 3 aren't optional garnish: feed governance bounds whether a hostile package can be there at all, and a short-lived, read-only token bounds what reading it buys. None of the three controls is sufficient alone.
- **BuildKit isn't automatic.** Secret mounts require the BuildKit frontend (`# syntax=docker/dockerfile:1`) and an enabled builder. An old builder will fail the mount — or worse, a "fix" that drops back to `ENV` silently re-leaks. The single enforcing build wrapper is what keeps "we use `--secret`" true instead of aspirational.
- **A private feed is a job, not a checkbox.** Someone mirrors upstream packages in, watches advisories, and curates versions. Governance has ongoing operational weight — the same honest cost as running an image-mirror pipeline. The trust it buys is real and it isn't free.
- **A lock file you don't enforce is decoration.** Reproducibility only holds if CI fails on drift and restores are actually locked. An unverified `packages.lock.json` is a comment, not a control.
- **Ephemeral tokens still need scoped identities.** A short-lived token minted by a CI identity that has broad rights to the whole feed estate is still a fat target for the minutes it lives. Turn the lifespan dial *and* scope the grant; don't let "it expires" excuse "it can read everything."

## The takeaway

The reason this small moment is worth a principal's attention is that it compresses the whole supply-chain argument into one `RUN` line: a credential, an artifact, and a boundary, all at once.

1. **See the feed as a governance boundary, not an annoyance.** It's private to control what enters your build, not to hide your code — and that control is what makes the dependencies trustable.
2. **Pin what the gate let in.** A lock file turns a curated dependency set into a reproducible, tamper-evident one. Curated *and* provable.
3. **Never let the token touch a layer.** `ENV` and `--build-arg` bake it into `docker inspect` and `docker history`, multi-stage or not. A BuildKit secret mount scopes it to one `RUN` on a tmpfs that's never committed.
4. **Mint the token, don't store it.** Treat CI as an identity that produces a short-lived credential at build time — provider-agnostic — instead of parking a static PAT in a pipeline variable.
5. **Close all three, because they backstop each other.** Acquire short-lived, pass without persisting, unlock something governed. Each gap is known and navigable; maturity is navigating all of them.

The build authenticating to a feed looked like a one-liner with a token in it. It's actually the place your supply chain is most exposed and most improvable at once — and the payoff for getting it right isn't just that the secret didn't leak. It's that you can now say, and prove, that every dependency in your image came through a gate you control. Private was never the goal. Trustable was.

_Closing these gaps with a different toolchain — OIDC to a cloud artifact store, a Nix or Bazel hermetic build, sigstore on your packages — or think I've over-weighted the governance angle? I'd genuinely like to compare notes. These designs get sharper in the open._
