At scale, engineering organizations find it difficult to keep healthy collaboration, knowledge sharing, and sustainable velocity. Standups find blockers quickly, but they do not give time to discuss technical details. Story grooming sessions focus on the business impact and the reason for the work. Implementation details come later, when someone starts the story. Sprint demos put working software in front of users. Users then tell the team if it built the right thing. Demos often intentionally ignore if the team built it correctly. Retrospectives give time for reflection. They often find opportunities for technical alignment, but their feedback can come late. A different ritual could help. It must be lightweight and scalable, and it must find technical detail, encourage mentorship, and support asynchronous learning without added overhead.

**Work in Progress (WIP) sessions** are that ritual. This simple but powerful ceremony changed how I led more than 50 engineers at Microsoft. It helped us scale knowledge sharing, reduce silos, and decrease bottlenecks.

## What Are Work in Progress Sessions?

WIP sessions are informal show-and-tells with no preparation. In them, engineers share technical details about their current work. These sessions are:

- **Lightweight:** No slides and no presentations. Only an honest walkthrough of your current work.
- **Technical:** Time to examine the how and why, from code patterns to tradeoffs.
- **Collaborative:** Opportunities for feedback and shared learning.
- **Inclusive:** Everyone can share and listen.

The goal is to **share knowledge, learn together, and grow as a team**.

## Why WIP Sessions Work

### 1. Surface Hidden Insights

In large teams, engineers make many technical decisions in silos. WIP sessions make those decisions visible. Engineers share their mental models, patterns, and also their difficulties. Technical debt then becomes a strategic tool instead of a hidden liability.

### 2. Normalize Technical Debt Discussions

WIP sessions do not treat technical debt as a bad word. They present it as a tradeoff that the team can manage. With this view, the team can iterate faster. Everyone agrees that the team will intentionally "pay back" the debt.

### 3. Build Mentorship & Trust

WIP creates a culture of openness. Junior engineers see how senior engineers think. Senior engineers see what junior team members work on. This exchange makes mentorship faster and raises the technical standard of the whole team.

### 4. Create Async Knowledge Flows

Record WIP sessions, or share summaries or notes from them. This creates a store of team knowledge. It supports asynchronous learning, which is ideal for distributed teams or different time zones.

## How I Led 50+ Engineers Through WIP

WIP sessions at scale need more than scheduled meetings. I made them work as follows:

- **Regular Cadence:** Sessions every week or every two weeks to keep momentum.
- **Balanced Participation:** I tracked who presented and from which teams. This made sure that representation was broad and prevented silos.
- **Demand vs Supply:** I monitored how many engineers wanted to present and how many did. This showed barriers, and I made adjustments.
- **Adaptive Format:** I changed the session format based on feedback: shorter slots, focused topics, or open Q&A.
- **Surface Key Insights:** I reported recurring themes or challenges to leadership for visibility and follow-up.

## Why You Should Consider Adding WIP

Does your team have these problems?

- Knowledge silos or communication bottlenecks
- Not enough technical mentorship or shared learning
- Difficulty balancing speed and code quality

If so, **Work in Progress sessions can be the missing ceremony that you need.**

They are easy to adopt, have low overhead, and have a large effect on engineering health and scale.

## Final Thoughts

To scale an engineering organization, processes and tools are not enough. Culture and communication matter. The WIP ceremony gave engineers a new channel to connect, mentor, and learn from each other. That channel was sustainable and scalable.

If your team has not tried it yet, try it. Sometimes, the simplest rituals give the biggest improvements.
